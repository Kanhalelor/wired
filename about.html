The solution

SignSight: a wearable that turns a sign into a spoken sentence.

A conceptual pair of smart glasses that watches, recognises, and speaks — designed so a Deaf or hard-of-hearing learner can express themselves in a mainstream classroom without waiting for an interpreter to be free.

Try it yourself
Click a hotspot on the glasses to read about that module, or run Start Demo Mode for a simulated recognition sequence.
Why glasses, why now

Communication support that travels with the learner, not the room.

Interpreters, captioners, and note-takers are enormously valuable — but they're a shared, scarce resource, tied to a room or a schedule. A wearable device flips that constraint: the support moves with the learner, into any classroom, any subject, any conversation in the corridor.

SignSight is our team's answer to a simple question we kept returning to during our research: what would it take for basic sign communication to be understood the instant it happens, without anyone having to stop and translate?

Design principles we held to

  • On-device first. Core recognition should not depend on a network connection or send a learner's data off the device.
  • Open-ear audio. Bone-conduction output was chosen deliberately so it never blocks ambient classroom sound or interferes with a hearing aid.
  • Small, honest vocabulary. We scoped the concept to a handful of high-value signs rather than overclaiming a full sign-language translator.
  • Everyday-wearable form factor. It should look and feel like a normal pair of glasses, not a medical device.
Under the hood

Four stages, explained.

01 — Capture

Miniature camera

A discreet, forward-facing camera on the upper frame captures the wearer's hand shape, orientation and motion — positioned out of the direct line of sight, targeting roughly a 90° wide-angle field of view at 30–60 fps in the conceptual spec.

02 — Recognise

On-device AI

A lightweight gesture-recognition model runs locally, matching motion patterns against a small demo vocabulary — Hello, Thank you, Yes, No, Help — with a target latency under one second per sign.

03 — Translate

Phrase mapping

Recognised signs map to short, natural-language phrases rather than a literal word-for-word gloss, so the output reads and sounds like something a person would actually say.

04 — Speak & display

Bone-conduction speaker + HUD caption

The phrase is spoken through an open-ear, bone-conduction transducer near the temple, while a subtle heads-up caption reinforces it visually for the wearer or people nearby.

What this prototype is — and isn't

SignSight, as it exists today, is a conceptual, non-commercial student prototype: a 3D model and simulated demo built to communicate a design direction, not a finished, clinically validated product. It recognises a small, fixed set of basic signs and is not a substitute for a qualified professional sign-language interpreter. We say this plainly because credibility with schools, policymakers, and the Deaf community starts with being honest about where the idea currently stands — and where we hope to take it with the right partners.
